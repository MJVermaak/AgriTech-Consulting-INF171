Adapting & Customising Information Systems

Fitting systems to smallholder farms, the processing plant and retail buyers, instead of forcing them to fit the software

Why one size does not fit

Most software is designed for a typical business: one office, reliable internet, staff who use computers daily. A smallholder supply chain is rarely typical.

Consider what a system for this partnership has to cope with:

  • Seasonality. Activity is concentrated in planting and harvest peaks, then quiet for months. Many systems assume steady, daily transactions.
  • Patchy connectivity. Farms may have weak signal and expensive data. A system that only works online will not be used in the field.
  • Mixed digital skills and languages. Members may be more comfortable in isiZulu, Sesotho, Xitsonga, Tshivenda or Afrikaans than in English, and many will use a basic smartphone or feature phone rather than a laptop.
  • Agricultural data structures. Plots instead of customers, varieties, planting and harvest dates, grades, batches that must be traced from farm to shelf.
  • Collective ownership. A cooperative has members, a committee, shared obligations and payment rules that standard accounting or sales software does not model.
  • Several organisations. The cooperative, the processor and the retailers each have their own systems, and each need to see different parts of the same information.

An information system that ignores these realities will be abandoned, or worse, will push people into awkward workarounds that corrupt the data. Adaptation is the work of closing the gap between what the software offers and what the organisation needs.

A warning from research. Davenport observed that enterprise systems carry assumptions about how an organisation should work, and that forcing a business to fit those assumptions can damage the things that make it competitive.[1] The reverse mistake is also common: customising so heavily that the system becomes expensive and fragile. Good adaptation sits between the two.

Your options: a spectrum

Adapting a system is not a single choice. Research on tailoring enterprise systems describes a spectrum of options, from changing nothing to changing the underlying code.[2] The table adapts these options to this partnership.

Adaptation options from lightest to heaviest
OptionWhat it meansAgri exampleProsCons
Use as is Adopt a ready-made product and change your own processes to match Using standard accounting software for the cooperative's payments Cheapest, quickest, well supported May not capture plots, grades or member rules
Configure Use settings the product already offers, with no new code Adding custom fields for crop variety and plot size; setting grade-based price lists; translating labels Low cost, upgrades keep working, vendor supports it Limited to what the product allows
Extend or integrate Add small separate tools linked to the product through its official connections (APIs) or data exports A simple offline mobile app for recording deliveries that sends records to the accounting system Fills specific gaps; core system stays untouched Needs technical skills; connections must be maintained
Customise Change the product's own code or logic Rewriting the payment module to handle shared cooperative deductions Fits exactly Costly; can break at every upgrade; can tie you to one developer
Build Develop a bespoke system A purpose-built traceability and planning platform for the whole partnership Complete control Highest cost and risk; you carry maintenance forever

A sensible rule of thumb: go only as far down the table as you must. Configure first, extend where the gap is real, and customise or build only for the few processes that truly make the partnership different. For a cooperative with limited budget and IT skills, the first three rows will usually cover almost everything.

Start with real needs

Adaptation goes wrong when it starts from the software's features. It goes right when it starts from the work people do and the problems they face. In this partnership each group needs something different from the same shared information.

Needs by stakeholder
StakeholderMain needsWhat the system should provide
Smallholder farmers Proof of what they delivered, fast and fair payment, simple tools, control over their own data Phone-based delivery receipts (including SMS or WhatsApp), payment statements in their language, clear view of only their own records
Cooperative managers and committee Member register, aggregate harvest view, payments, reporting to members and funders Member and plot register, dashboards, payment calculation with cooperative deductions, exportable reports
Processing facility Predictable volumes, quality assurance, batch traceability, capacity planning Harvest forecasts, intake scheduling, grading records and batch numbers that link back to farms
Retailers Reliable supply, food safety and origin evidence, delivery times Traceability reports, delivery tracking and the quality records buyers and certification schemes expect

How to find out what people need

  • Watch the work. Spend a day at a delivery point and at the plant's intake bay. Notice every piece of paper, call and workaround.
  • Talk to farmers in groups and individually. Some people will not raise problems in front of the committee or the processor.
  • Map the process. Draw each step from planting to payment and mark where information is lost, retyped or disputed.
  • Separate needs from wants. Rank requirements as essential, important or nice to have. Customise only for the essentials that no standard option can meet.
  • Test with a prototype. Let a few farmers try a simple version before anything is built in full. Involving users early is one of the most consistent findings in systems implementation.

What can be adapted

Almost every system can be adapted in six areas. Thinking in these areas helps you ask suppliers precise questions.

1. Data structure: what the system records

Standard systems record customers, products and invoices. This supply chain also needs farmers, plots (with size and GPS location), crops and varieties, planting and expected harvest dates, grades and batches. Ask whether you can add your own fields and relationships without programming. Linking each delivery to a farm and plot is what makes traceability possible later. See Data Management for why consistent structure matters.

2. Interface: how people use it

For farmers this usually means offline-first mobile use that stores entries on the phone and syncs when signal returns, simple screens with icons and large buttons, and local language options. Where smartphones are scarce, SMS or USSD menus or a WhatsApp-based flow can work for receipts and alerts. Test with real users on the devices they own.

3. Workflows: how work moves through the system

A delivery might need to flow like this: farmer declares expected harvest, collection is scheduled, the load is weighed, the processor grades it, the farmer is notified, and payment is approved and paid. Check whether the product lets you set the steps, approvals and notifications to match, including who must approve a payment or a rejected load.

4. Reports and dashboards: what people see

Each group needs a different view. A farmer needs a clear statement of their deliveries and payments. The committee needs member and crop totals. The processor needs forecast intake by week. Retailers need origin and quality evidence. Reports should be configurable without a developer where possible.

5. Roles and permissions: who sees what

This is where adaptation meets privacy. A well-adapted system lets each farmer see only their own records, lets the committee see member-level information needed for its role, and lets the processor and retailers see only the aggregate or traceability data they are entitled to. These rules should reflect what farmers have agreed to, and should be set up in line with POPIA. See Ethics & POPIA.

6. Integration: how it connects to other systems

The cooperative's tool must exchange data with the processor's enterprise system and perhaps the retailers' ordering systems. Decide early what is exchanged, in which format, how often, and who is responsible when the connection fails. See Enterprise Systems for the integration challenges.

Worked example: a delivery recording app

This is a hypothetical but realistic example of adaptation through configuring and extending, instead of customising.

Problem. A cooperative of about 60 farmers delivers vegetables to a processor. Weights are written on paper slips at the weighbridge, retyped later, and regularly disputed. Payments are weeks late. The processor wants farm-level traceability for its retail customers.

From problem to adapted solution
StepDecisionReason
1. Map the process Interview farmers, weighbridge staff and the finance clerk; draw the delivery-to-payment flow Exposes that errors enter at retyping, not at weighing
2. Choose the route Choose an existing accounting package plus a low-cost offline data-collection app, rather than building a new system Budget is small; a ready-made tool covers 80% of the need
3. Configure Add farmer ID, plot, crop variety and grade fields; set the price list by grade; set labels in the local language Captures what traceability needs without code
4. Extend Weighbridge staff record deliveries on a tablet, which prints or sends a receipt and syncs to the accounts system Removes retyping; farmers hold proof of delivery immediately
5. Set permissions Farmers see only their own records; the processor sees batch and farm origin for traceability but not members' finances Limits data use to the purpose agreed with members
6. Pilot Run the new method for one crop and 10 farmers for a season, alongside paper Finds problems cheaply and builds champions
7. Review and roll out Compare disputes, payment speed and data errors with the old method; adjust and extend to all members Decisions are based on evidence

What was not customised. The accounting package's code stayed untouched. Everything the cooperative needed was achieved with settings and a small linked app, so upgrades will not break the system.

Costs and trade-offs

Every adaptation has a price beyond the initial work. Weigh these before you agree to change a system.

Trade-offs of heavy adaptation
Trade-offExplanationPractical response
Upgrade problems Changes to a product's code may stop working when the vendor releases updates, forcing repeated rework Prefer configuration and integrations over code changes
Developer dependence If one person built it, the organisation depends on that person to maintain it Require documentation and source code ownership in the contract
Total cost of ownership Licences, hosting, support, training, data and later changes cost more than the purchase price Calculate costs over three to five years
Complexity The more customised the system, the harder it is to train new staff and to fix Keep to the essentials; remove unused features
Vendor lock-in Custom features may only work in one product, making it hard to switch Require data export in open formats such as CSV
Speed versus fit Waiting for a perfect fit can delay benefits for a whole season Start with a minimum workable version, improve in stages

Customisation can create technical debt. A quick change that works today can make every future change slower and more expensive. Before approving a customisation, ask: "Could we change our process or use a setting instead?"

Getting people to use it

A perfectly adapted system that nobody uses has failed. Research on technology acceptance finds that people adopt a system mainly when they believe it is useful for their work and easy to use.[3] Diffusion research adds that people are more likely to try something they can observe working for others first.[4] Both ideas shape how to introduce systems into a farming community.

  • Show the benefit to the farmer first. Quick receipts and faster payments are more convincing than reports for the processor.
  • Use respected champions. Choose trusted farmers and extension officers to pilot and demonstrate.
  • Train in practice, in the local language, at times that suit the farming calendar, and provide a simple help line or contact person.
  • Run old and new together for a while. Parallel running builds confidence and exposes errors before payments depend on the system.
  • Listen and visibly act on feedback. When farmers see their suggestions change the system, trust grows.
  • Plan for turnover. Document processes so a new clerk or committee member can learn them.

Structured change models such as ADKAR break this into awareness, desire, knowledge, ability and reinforcement.[5] They are a useful checklist: do people understand why the change is happening, want it, know how to use it, are able to do it in practice, and are supported to keep doing it?

Governing change

Systems keep changing after launch. Without agreed rules, small unrecorded changes accumulate until nobody knows how the system works.

  • Name a system owner from the business, not only IT, who is accountable for the system serving its purpose.
  • Use a simple change process: request, assess need and cost, decide, test, record. Keep a log.
  • Include all parties in decisions that affect shared data. A change that gives the processor more access to farmer data should be agreed with the cooperative and its members.
  • Review yearly. Ask what is used, what is ignored, what workarounds have appeared and what should be removed.

Checklist before you customise

Open the checklist
  • Have we described the problem in terms of the work, not the software?
  • Have farmers, staff at the plant and buyers all been consulted?
  • Could a setting, a change of process, or an existing product feature solve it?
  • Does the change affect personal information, and have we considered POPIA and members' agreement?
  • What will it cost over three to five years, including support and upgrades?
  • Who will maintain it and is it documented?
  • Can we get our data out in an open format if we change supplier?
  • Does it work offline and in the languages our users need?
  • Have we piloted it with real users before rolling out?
  • How will we measure whether it worked?

Key takeaways

  • Systems built for typical businesses often do not fit seasonal, multi-party smallholder supply chains.
  • Adaptation is a spectrum. Configure first, extend where needed, and customise only the essentials.
  • Begin with the real work and real needs of farmers, the cooperative, the processor and retailers.
  • Roles and permissions are an ethical decision as well as a technical one.
  • Heavy customisation brings costs that last for years.
  • A system succeeds when people use it, which depends on usefulness, ease of use, training and trust.
